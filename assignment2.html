Project Overview

Loop Together explores how a browser-based audio instrument can make sound creation approachable for beginners. Users experiment with microphone input, recorded samples and repetition through simple interfaces.

These six parallel prototypes explore two design areas: looping and sampling interaction, and feedback. Each group contains three alternatives that investigate different ways of supporting sound exploration.

The prototypes explore individual interactions that could support future social sound-making. They do not implement shared online recording sessions.

Area 1 — Looping and Sampling Interaction

This area directly explores my extended technique of looping and sampling. I investigate how familiar buttons, a circular display and selectable steps can help users understand and control repetition.

The aim is to support rhythm-making without requiring musical notation. Each version provides a different relationship between user actions and the timing of sound.

PROTOTYPE 1A

Button Loop

Users record a short sound and repeat the complete sample using clearly labelled media controls. The recording length determines the loop length.

This design explores a familiar starting point for beginners. It lets users focus on recording and listening before introducing control over individual beats.

Open Prototype 1A

PROTOTYPE 1B

Circular Loop

Eight positions form a circular cycle. A highlight moves around the circle, and the recorded sample restarts at the beginning of each cycle.

The circular layout represents repetition as returning to a starting point. It explores whether movement helps users follow the cycle and anticipate the sample restarting.

Open Prototype 1B

PROTOTYPE 1C

Step Loop

Users select positions in an eight-step grid to choose when their sample is triggered. Selected steps appear green, while an outline shows the playback position.

This design explores more direct control over rhythm. Turning steps on and off lets users experiment with the placement of sound within a repeating pattern.

Open Prototype 1C

Area 2 — Feedback and Guidance

This area explores how the interface communicates microphone activity and system states. Feedback can help users recognise that an input has been received and understand what they can do next.

The alternatives focus on a level display, written instructions and expressive animation. They investigate feedback as confirmation, guidance and encouragement to try different sounds.

PROTOTYPE 2A

Visual Feedback

A meter and pulsing circle respond to microphone input. Bar length and circle size provide visible feedback when users speak, clap or tap.

This design explores how simple visual changes can confirm sound activity. It shows relative changes, rather than a calibrated sound-level measurement.

Open Prototype 2A

PROTOTYPE 2B

Text Feedback

Short instructions guide users through recording, stopping and playing a sample. Messages describe the current state and the next available action.

This version explores explicit guidance for beginners. Disabled buttons support the instructions by showing which actions are currently available.

Open Prototype 2B

PROTOTYPE 2C

Expressive Feedback

Animated bars respond to frequency information from the microphone. Different sounds create changing patterns across the display.

This version explores how animation can encourage experimentation with voice, claps and taps. Its focus is live visual feedback rather than recording and playback.

Open Prototype 2C

Shared Design Choices

The prototypes share a dark background, green controls and clear labels. Consistent styling helps focus attention on the interaction differences between versions.

Status messages explain changes such as recording and playback. In the step sequencer, selected positions and the current playback position have different visual states.

Peer User Testing

The testing session explores how participants understand the prototypes, what they expect to happen and where they need more guidance.

Testing Tasks

  1. In 1A, record a short sound, start the loop and stop it.
  2. In 1B, record a sound and follow one circular cycle.
  3. In 1C, select three steps, play the sequence and change one selected step.
  4. In 2A, compare the visual response to a quiet sound and a louder sound.
  5. In 2B, follow the instructions to record and play a sample.
  6. In 2C, try a voice sound, a clap and a tap. Observe how the bars change.

Please note that 2A and 2C focus on live input feedback, while 2B includes recording and playback. Consider this difference when comparing the experiences.
